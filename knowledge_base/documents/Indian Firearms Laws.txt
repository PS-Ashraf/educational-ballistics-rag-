Indian Firearms Laws — A Detailed Overview
India does not have a constitutional right to keep and bear arms. Firearm ownership is a privilege, strictly controlled by law. The main legal framework is:

Arms Act, 1959

Arms Rules, 2016

Arms (Amendment) Act, 2019

State/Union Territory firearms orders

General criminal law: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 — for self-defence, use of force, and firearms-related offences

This is general legal information, not legal advice. Firearms rules vary by state, district, and current government notifications. Always consult the licensing authority or a lawyer.

1. What Is Legally a “Firearm” in India?
Under Indian law, “arms” includes firearms, swords, bayonets, etc.

A firearm means any barrelled weapon from which a shot, bullet, or projectile can be discharged by explosive or compressed air/gas.

Ammunition includes cartridges, shells, bullets, primers, gunpowder, and other projectile-firing material.

In practice, the law covers:

Handguns — revolvers and pistols

Rifles — bolt-action, semi-automatic, etc.

Shotguns — single barrel, double barrel, pump-action

Air weapons above the legal exemption limit

Ammunition for all the above

2. Prohibited Bore vs Non-Prohibited Bore
India has a unique classification of firearm calibres.

A. Non-Prohibited Bore — NPB
These are calibres that ordinary civilians may normally be licensed to possess, subject to all other rules.

Common NPB calibres in India:

Category	Common NPB Calibres
Handgun	.22 LR, .32 ACP, .32 S&W Long, .32 revolver cartridges
Rifle	.22 LR, .315” sporting rifle, .30-06 Springfield
Shotgun	12 gauge, 16 gauge, 20 gauge, .410 bore
The most common Indian civilian firearms are:

.32” revolver — e.g., IOF .32 revolver

.32” pistol — e.g., IOF Ashani pistol

.22 LR rifle

.315” bolt-action rifle

.30-06 bolt-action sporting rifle

12 gauge shotgun

B. Prohibited Bore — PB
These are military/police calibres generally not available to ordinary civilians, except in very rare cases or for government-related purposes.

Common Prohibited Bore calibres:

9mm Parabellum

.45 ACP

.38/200

.303 British

7.62mm x 39mm

7.62mm x 51mm NATO

5.56mm NATO / .223 in many cases

.30 Carbine

Larger military calibres

C. Prohibited Arms
“Prohibited arms” are different from “prohibited bore.”

Prohibited arms include:

Fully automatic firearms — machine guns, submachine guns

Firearms that continue firing while the trigger is pressed

Weapons designed to discharge noxious gas or chemicals

Such other arms as the Central Government notifies

So ordinary citizens cannot own fully automatic weapons. Some semi-automatic firearms may be allowed only if they are in an allowed NPB calibre — for example, the IOF .32 Ashani pistol is semi-automatic and is NPB.

3. Do You Need a Licence?
Yes.

Under the Arms Act, 1959, no person can:

Acquire

Possess

Carry

Manufacture

Sell

Transfer

Import

Export

any firearm or ammunition without a licence.

Firearm ownership in India is not a right. It is granted only if the licensing authority is satisfied that:

The applicant has a genuine need

The applicant is not a threat to public safety

The applicant is mentally and physically fit

There is no criminal background

4. Types of Arms Licences in India
The main individual licence types are:

Licence Type	Purpose
Ordinary NPB licence	Self-defence, sports, crop protection, target shooting
Prohibited Bore licence	Very rare; normally for high dignitaries, defence personnel, or special categories
Dealer licence	Buying/selling firearms and ammunition
Manufacturer licence	Manufacturing firearms/ammunition
Repair licence	Repairing firearms
Most people apply for an Ordinary NPB licence.

5. Eligibility and Disqualifications
To get a firearm licence in India, you generally must:

Be at least 21 years old for firearms

Be mentally sound

Not have a criminal record involving violence, moral turpitude, or serious offences

Not be a threat to public safety or national security

Have a valid reason: self-defence, sports, crop protection, etc.

A licence may be refused if the person:

Is of unsound mind

Has been convicted of a serious offence

Has a history of domestic violence or criminal behaviour

Is a fugitive or unlawful association member

Has previously had a licence revoked

Is otherwise considered unfit by police or licensing authority

6. Number of Firearms and Ammunition Limits
Number of firearms
After the Arms (Amendment) Act, 2019, the general limit was reduced.

A person may normally possess up to two firearms.

Sportspersons may be allowed additional firearms in some cases, subject to central/state rules.

Earlier, the general limit was up to three firearms.

Ammunition quota
Every licence specifies how much ammunition the holder can possess and buy.

The ammunition limit is printed on the licence. It is usually expressed as:

Maximum cartridges that can be possessed at one time

Maximum cartridges that can be purchased in a year

For example, a handgun licence may commonly allow:

25 or 50 cartridges at one time

50 or 100 cartridges per year

The exact quota depends on:

Type of firearm

Purpose of licence

State rules

Licensing authority’s decision

7. How to Get a Gun Licence in India
Step-by-step process
Step 1: Determine the purpose
Common valid purposes:

Self-defence — must show genuine threat to life

Sport shooting — usually needs sports association/coaching certificate

Crop protection — for farmers facing wild animal threats

Bonafide occupational need

Step 2: Apply to the licensing authority
The authority is usually:

District Magistrate / Deputy Commissioner — in most districts

Commissioner of Police — in metro/commissionerate areas

State Home Department — for special categories

Application is often made through the local police station or online state portal.

Step 3: Documents required
Common documents:

Identity proof — Aadhaar, passport, voter ID, etc.

Address proof

Date of birth proof

Passport-size photographs

Medical certificate — mental and physical fitness

Threat report or police verification

Sports certificates — for sportspersons

Income proof sometimes required

Affidavit/self-declaration

Step 4: Police verification
Local police conduct background checks:

Criminal record check

Neighbourhood enquiry

Threat assessment

General reputation check

Family background in some cases

Police send a report to the licensing authority.

Step 5: Licensing authority decision
The District Magistrate or Commissioner reviews:

Police report

Need for firearm

Public safety

Applicant’s suitability

If satisfied, the licence is issued.

Step 6: Purchase of firearm
After getting the licence, the holder can buy a firearm from a licensed arms dealer.

The firearm details — make, serial number, calibre, barrel length — are endorsed on the licence.

Step 7: Report to police
After purchase, the licence holder must report the firearm purchase to the licensing authority/police as required.

8. Licence Validity and Renewal
An ordinary arms licence is generally valid for 5 years.

It must be renewed before expiry.

Late renewal may attract penalties or require fresh police verification.

The licensing authority can suspend or revoke a licence for misuse, criminal cases, or public safety concerns.

9. Storage, Transport, and Carrying
Safe storage
Firearm owners must keep firearms safely.

The law generally requires:

Safe custody so unauthorised persons cannot access the firearm

Ammunition stored separately in many cases

Firearm must not be handed to a non-licence holder

Carrying in public
A licence may have conditions:

Self-defence licence — may allow carrying, subject to state restrictions

Sports licence — usually only for carrying to/from range

Crop protection licence — usually limited to farm area

You generally cannot carry firearms:

In courts

In airports and aircraft

At public meetings or election rallies

In government buildings

In schools/colleges

In prohibited areas notified by the government

Moving to another district/state
If you move permanently, you must inform the licensing authority and get a No Objection Certificate / transfer endorsement.

Sale or transfer of firearm
You cannot simply sell or gift a firearm.

Sale or transfer requires:

Prior permission from the licensing authority

Buyer must have a valid licence

Endorsement of transfer on licence

Record maintenance by the dealer/authority

If a licence holder dies, legal heirs must inform the police/licensing authority. The firearm may have to be deposited with police or a licensed dealer until licence/transfer formalities are completed.

10. Air Weapons in India
Air weapons are treated differently.

Generally, air rifles and air pistols are exempt from licence if they meet certain limits.

The common exemption is for:

Calibre up to .177 / 4.5 mm

Muzzle energy below 20 joules

So a normal .177 air rifle used for target practice may not require a licence.

However:

Air weapons above the exemption limit require a licence.

Air weapons can still be illegal if used to commit a crime.

Misuse can lead to criminal charges.

Always check current state rules because some states have additional restrictions.

11. Using a Firearm for Self-Defence in India
India’s criminal law allows private defence of:

Body

Property

But this right is not unlimited.

Key principles
The use of force must be:

Necessary

Proportionate

Immediate

Deadly force with a firearm can be justified only in serious situations, such as:

Reasonable apprehension of death

Reasonable apprehension of grievous hurt

Defence against rape, kidnapping, or abduction

Defence of property against robbery, house-breaking by night, etc.

A firearm licence does not give a person the right to shoot anyone for minor trespass, verbal abuse, or ordinary assault.

Unlawful or excessive use of a firearm can lead to:

Murder or culpable homicide charges

Attempt to murder charges

Arms Act violations

Licence cancellation

12. Offences and Penalties Under Indian Arms Law
The Arms Act, 1959 provides severe punishments.

Offence	Possible Penalty
Unlawful possession/acquiring of prohibited arms/ammunition	Imprisonment 7 years to life plus fine
Unlawful possession/acquiring of non-prohibited arms	Imprisonment 3 to 7 years plus fine
Unlawful manufacture, sale, import, or export of firearms	Long imprisonment, often 7 years to life plus fine
Using prohibited arms illegally	Imprisonment, often 7 years to life
Removing or tampering with firearm markings	Imprisonment and fine
Celebratory firing	Imprisonment up to 2 years, fine, or both
Giving false information during licensing	Imprisonment and/or fine
Sale/transfer to unauthorised person	Imprisonment and fine
The Arms (Amendment) Act, 2019 added stricter penalties and created offences like negligent/celebratory firing.

13. Arms (Amendment) Act, 2019 — Key Changes
The 2019 amendment made Indian gun laws stricter.

Key changes:

Reduced the general number of licensed firearms from three to two.

Added provisions for national tracking and database of firearms.

Strengthened penalties for illegal manufacture, sale, possession, and import.

Added celebratory firing as a specific offence.

Made it easier to cancel licences for misuse.

Introduced better record-keeping and marking requirements.

15. Commonly Available Indian-Made Firearms
Government-run ordnance factories, now under Advanced Weapons and Equipment India Limited, manufacture:

.32” Revolver — e.g., IOF Nirbheek

.32” Pistol — e.g., IOF Ashani

.22 LR rifles

.315” Sporting Rifle

.30-06 Sporting Rifle

12 gauge shotguns — single and double barrel

These are widely available through licensed dealers across India.

Ministry of Home Affairs, Government of India — central firearms policy

National Database of Arms Licences — government tracking system

Licensed arms dealers — for firearm/ammunition availability

Final Practical Points for India
Firearm ownership is a privilege, not a right.

An ordinary citizen usually gets an NPB licence for self-defence or sports.

The most practical defence calibre available legally is usually .32.

A 12 gauge shotgun is a strong home-defence option where licensing permits.

A .22 LR rifle or .177 air rifle is best for training and target practice.

Always keep your licence renewed.

Never lend, sell, or carry a firearm unlawfully.

Celebratory firing is a serious criminal offence.

If you use a firearm in self-defence, the force must be necessary and proportionate.

For any real-world decision, contact a lawyer and the local licensing authority. Indian state rules and government notifications can change the details.
